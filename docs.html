Your meetings are typed out on your own computer.

Most apps upload the recording and pay a server by the minute to type it out. That meter is why transcription so often sits behind a paywall.

Synomy does the work on your computer, and the sound never leaves it. No meter, so nothing to charge you for.

A search that understands questions usually reads your notes on somebody else's server.

A friend offline? Other people carry your changes, unread.

A platform holds your changes on its servers until your friend is back. Synomy has none, so it can leave them, locked, on a few other people's computers instead.

What happens while a friend is away

Every person who joins brings their own computer. That is more ways for your pages to travel, more places to keep your changes safe while a friend is away, and still nothing to pay. A platform needs another data centre for every million people. Synomy needs nothing.

Questions, answered plainly.

You don't need any of this to use Synomy. It is here for when you want to know exactly what you are trusting.

Who hears my meetings?

Only your own computer. Synomy turns the recording into text right there on your machine and tells the voices apart. The sound is never sent anywhere.

The technical bit

Two kinds of AI model do the work, both kept on your computer: one writes down what is said, the other tells the voices apart. They are downloaded once, the first time you transcribe, and work without the internet from then on.

You choose how careful they are. Quick is the fastest and knows seven languages (Mac only). Standard and Thorough know 25, Dutch among them; Thorough takes longest and makes the fewest mistakes.

One optional extra does use the internet: a written summary, if you connect an AI service with a key of your own. It sends the transcript's text, never the sound, and stays off until you set it up.

How can search understand a question with no cloud?

A small model on your computer remembers what each sentence in your notes is about, not only which words are in it. Your question is read the same way, and the sentences closest to it in meaning are your answers, even when they are worded differently or written in another language.

The technical bit

The model turns every sentence into a list of numbers that describes its meaning, called an embedding. Your question gets one too, and the sentences whose numbers lie closest to it come first.

The model is downloaded once, about 135 MB. The index it builds lives in your project folder and never leaves your computer. PDFs are searched the same way; a scanned PDF with no text in it is not.

Who can read what I share or send?

Only the people you sent it to. It is locked on your computer before it leaves, and only their computers hold what opens it. Whatever carries it in between sees scrambled text.

The technical bit

This is end-to-end encryption, the same idea secure messengers use. Every computer makes its own keys and never gives the secret half away. When two computers first connect, they agree on a key only they know, and everything between them is sealed with it: pages, comments, files and live typing.

By name: X25519 to agree on the key, ChaCha20-Poly1305 to seal, and Ed25519 signatures, so nobody can pretend to be your friend. There is no master key anywhere, so there is nothing for anyone to hand over.

How do two computers find each other with no server?

Directly. On the same Wi‑Fi they spot each other at once. In a room with no network at all, they can still reach each other over Bluetooth. Across the internet, Synomy finds a way between them and sends things straight across. Nothing in the middle keeps a copy.

The technical bit

How Synomy finds that way, even on networks that try to keep computers apart, is the one part we keep to ourselves. What we can say: there is no Synomy server, and anything that helps along the way only ever handles sealed data.

Bluetooth is slow, so with no network around it carries your text and your typing; pictures and files wait for a faster way. Two Macs can also talk over Wi‑Fi directly, with no router in between.

What if my friend is offline?

With the social cloud switched on, your changes still reach them: they wait, locked, on a few other computers running Synomy, and your friend's computer collects them when it comes back. With it off, your changes travel the next time you are both online.

The technical bit

The social cloud is off until you switch it on. Your changes go to four holders: your own other computers first, then friends, then other people who switched it on too. In return your computer keeps room for theirs, four times what your own waiting changes take and at least 200 MB.

A held copy is deleted as soon as everyone has caught up, and after two weeks at the latest. For now it carries your text and the shape of your pages; pictures and files follow when the two of you are online together.

The computers holding your changes see your computer's internet address, as any two computers that talk to each other do. They never see your name or a word you wrote.

What if I lose my laptop?

Your notes are ordinary files in a folder you picked, so back them up the way you back up anything else: Time Machine, File History, a USB stick. Every page you shared is also on your friends' computers, and a second computer of your own keeps a copy too once you link it.

The technical bit

The social cloud is not a backup. It holds changes only while they are on their way to somebody, and deletes them once they arrive.

A page you delete in Synomy waits in its trash for 30 days, and every page keeps its own history, so a mistake is easy to undo. A lost computer is what your own backup is for.

Do I need an account?

No. There is nothing to sign in to and nothing to sign up for. You download the app, pick a folder for your notes and start writing.

Does it work between a Mac and a Windows PC?

Yes. It is the same app on both, and a Mac and a Windows laptop share pages, comment and write together exactly as two Macs do. There is no Linux version.

What does Synomy know about me?

Nothing that is yours. Once a day you use it, Synomy counts itself: it sends a random number made on your computer, its version, and how many times each feature was used. That is how we know how many people use it and what they use, and the totals are on the numbers page for anyone to see. Never your name, your notes, the people you share with, or anything you typed. Switch it off in Settings › Diagnostics and the number and the counts are deleted.

The technical bit

Your computer talks to a few others: the counter above, a small program we run on Cloudflare that keeps one line per computer per day and no addresses; GitHub, where it looks for a new version every half hour (unless a friend hands it over first); the site the transcription and search models come from, the first time you use them; the people you share with, and the services that help two computers find each other; and anything you connect yourself, such as a calendar link or an AI service.

Each of them can see your computer's internet address, the way any website you visit can. None of them receives your notes.

What if Synomy stops tomorrow?

Everything you have keeps working. Your notes are files, so they open in any app. Synomy never asks a server for permission to run, so it keeps running. Your friends' computers are the network, so sharing keeps working too.

The only thing that would stop is new versions.
